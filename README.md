# Designing the Earner Experience

*From claim rates to career use.*

## About this course

**Course ID:** ICB-P13

**Who it is for.** This course is for learning and development and corporate academy leads, programme and micro-credential leads, and learning designers and teaching staff. You may work in a university, a college, a training provider, a corporate academy or an HR department. You already know how earners receive, claim and share a credential. Now you will design what happens around that moment.

**Framework alignment.** The course supports the ICoBC Credentialing Skills Framework (ICSF), skill EXPR "Earner experience and agency" (VRITE pillar: Experience), level L3 "Enable", stage A "Ability". At this level you design earner journeys and support for a programme, and you improve claim and share rates using evidence. At the Ability stage you practise on realistic tasks in a controlled setting. You work on cases and on a programme of your own choice.

**Prerequisite.** ICB-F03 Earn, Claim and Share Your Credentials.

**Learning outcomes.** After this course you can:

1. Map the earner journey from enrolment to career use.
2. Find drop-off points using platform data and earner research.
3. Design communication and support that raise claim and share rates.
4. Test changes and measure their effect.

**Workload.** This is a workshop with 6 hours of notional learning. This file covers the self-paced part. Live and workshop elements appear as facilitator notes in each module. Facilitators decide how to split the time between self-paced work and live sessions.

**How it is assessed.** You complete practice activities in each module. A final knowledge check (10 single-choice questions) tests your understanding. The main assessment is a journey map and an improvement plan with metrics. A trained assessor marks this task with the rubric in the section "Assessment and badge". This file does not grade it.

**Badge.** "Designing the Earner Experience (Ability, L3)", alignment code ICSF-EXPR-L3-A. The badge shows that you completed these tasks in a controlled setting. It does not prove competence in any job.

## Journey mapping

### Why the earner journey needs a map

An earner does not experience your credential as one event. The earner experiences a series of steps over weeks or months. Each step can go well or badly. If you design only the issuing step, you miss most of the experience.

In this course the **earner journey** runs from enrolment to career use. A typical journey has these stages:

1. **Enrol.** The person joins the programme and learns that a credential is part of it.
2. **Learn and assess.** The person completes the work. The credential may be mentioned only now and then.
3. **Earn.** The person meets the criteria and the credential is issued.
4. **Notice.** The person receives a message about the credential and sees it.
5. **Claim.** The person accepts the credential and, if needed, creates an account or connects a wallet.
6. **Share.** The person adds the credential to a profile, a CV, a portfolio or an email.
7. **Use.** The person uses the credential in a real setting: a job application, a promotion discussion, a client proposal, or an application to further study.

Claim rate and share rate sit in the middle of this journey. They depend on what happened before, such as whether the earner knew about the credential early, and on what the earner expects afterwards, such as whether anyone will look at it.

### Building a journey map

A journey map is a working document. It shows what the earner does, thinks and feels at each stage, and what your organisation does in return. A useful map has four rows:

- **Earner action.** What the person does at this stage.
- **Earner question or worry.** What the person wants to know or fears.
- **Organisation touchpoint.** The message, page, person or tool the earner meets.
- **Evidence.** What data or research tells you how this stage performs.

Fill in the map from the earner's side first. Many teams start from their own process and describe what the organisation does. That produces a map of internal tasks and hides the earner's experience.

Include people beyond the earner. A line manager may need to approve time for learning. A tutor may need to confirm results. An employer may need to understand the credential. Each of these people can help or block the earner at a stage.

Keep the map to one page. If you need more, you are mapping several journeys. Different earner groups often have different journeys. A first-year student on a degree programme and a mid-career employee on a short course may share the same credential and still have very different steps.

### Earner agency at each stage

The skill EXPR is called "Earner experience and agency". Agency means the earner decides what to do with the credential. The earner chooses whether to claim it, where to store it, and who can see it.

Good design supports these choices. It does not push earners into sharing. Check each stage with these questions:

- Does the earner know what they will receive and what it can be used for?
- Can the earner say no, or wait, without losing the credential?
- Does the earner control who sees the credential and how much of it?
- Are the steps clear enough for someone who has never used a credential platform?

A programme that pressures earners into public sharing may raise a share rate on paper and harm trust. Keep this in mind when you set goals later in the course.

**Worked example: Two journeys, two sets of drop-offs**

*University context.* A university continuing education centre issues a micro-credential after a 10-week course in data literacy. The programme lead, Amara, maps the journey with a short survey and three conversations with recent graduates. She finds that most earners first hear about the credential in the final week. Earners say they thought it was an internal certificate that only the university would recognise. The map shows a gap at stage 2: the credential is never explained during the course. She marks stage 2 and stage 4 as likely causes of weak claim rates.

*Corporate academy context.* A corporate academy at a manufacturing company issues a credential for completing a safety leadership programme. The L&D lead, Jonas, maps the journey with HR and a sample of line managers. The map shows that earners receive the credential by email on a Friday afternoon, usually after a shift. Managers do not know the credential exists, so nobody asks about it in appraisals. Stage 7 is weak because the use setting is missing. Jonas marks stage 4 (timing of the message) and stage 7 (manager awareness) as places to investigate.

*Both examples are illustrative. The findings are hypotheses to check with data, which is the focus of the next module.*

**Practice activity: Draft a journey map for your programme**

Choose a real programme you work with or a programme you know well. If you have none, use the university or corporate case above and invent plausible details, labelled as assumptions.

1. Write the seven stages as column headings.
2. For each stage, write one earner action and one earner question or worry.
3. Add one organisation touchpoint per stage. Write "none" if there is none.
4. Mark where you think the earner may leave the journey. Add a short reason for each mark.
5. Add at least one person other than the earner (for example a manager or tutor) and note how they affect one stage.
6. Check agency: list one point where the earner makes a choice and note whether the earner has enough information to choose.

Keep this draft. You will refine it in later modules and use it in the final task.

## Data and earner research

### Reading platform data

Platform data shows what earners did. It does not show why. Use data to find where the journey weakens, then use research to understand the reasons.

Most credential platforms and learning systems can report some or all of these measures. Check what yours can export before you plan.

- **Issued.** The number of credentials issued in a period.
- **Delivered.** The number of messages that reached an inbox, if reported.
- **Opened or viewed.** The number of earners who opened the message or the credential page.
- **Claimed or accepted.** The number of earners who completed the claim step.
- **Shared.** The number of earners who shared the credential through a link, a social network or an export.
- **Verified views.** The number of times someone other than the earner viewed or verified the credential, if reported.

From these counts you calculate rates. A **claim rate** is claimed divided by issued. A **share rate** is shared divided by claimed. Define each rate in writing before you compare numbers. Teams often disagree because one person divides by issued and another by delivered.

Add two more details to every rate: the **time window** (for example, 30 days after issue) and the **group** (for example, one cohort or one programme). A claim rate with no window is hard to read, because earners can claim months later.

Data has limits. Platform logs may not capture a share that happens outside the platform, such as a screenshot pasted into a CV. A low share count can reflect missing tracking. Open Badges 3.0 and CLR 2.0 are the current specifications for these credentials, and platform support for them is still uneven. Check the conformance certification of your tools and ask your supplier which events they record. Status as of November 2026; check before publishing.

### Finding the weak points

Lay the counts along the journey and calculate the step-to-step rate between stages. The biggest fall between two neighbouring stages points to a drop-off. Do not compare against a "good" figure from elsewhere. Programmes differ in audience, purpose and platform, and public benchmarks may not fit yours. Compare against your own earlier cohorts and against the goal you set.

### Earner research

Data points to a place. Earner research explains the cause. Use small, quick methods. You do not need a large study to learn useful things.

- **Short interviews.** Talk to five to eight earners for 15 to 20 minutes. Include earners who claimed and earners who did not. Ask what they did after they heard about the credential, and what stopped them.
- **Short surveys.** Ask three to five questions. Use plain words. Offer an "I did not know about it" option.
- **Walkthroughs.** Ask an earner to claim a test credential while you watch and say nothing. Note every pause, error and question.
- **Support tickets and emails.** Read the messages earners send to support. Group them by theme.
- **Stakeholder interviews.** Ask tutors, managers and employers what they know about the credential and what they do with it.

Keep to ethical practice. Tell people why you are asking and how you will use their answers. Collect only what you need. Follow your organisation's data protection rules and the applicable law, for example the GDPR in the European Union. Do not name individual earners in reports without their consent.

A simple way to join data and research is a **drop-off table**. For each weak point, write what the data shows, what research suggests as the cause, how sure you are, and what you would need to learn next.

**Worked example: From funnel to likely cause**

*College context.* A college offers a short employability credential to adult learners. The programme lead, Leila, reviews data for one cohort. Of 120 credentials issued, 30 were claimed within 30 days. Opening rate is about 70 per cent. She notes that most earners opened the message and then did not claim. She watches three earners claim a test credential. All three stop at the account creation step, and two say they do not want another password. She writes in her drop-off table: "Weak point B. Data: high open, low claim. Research: account creation causes stops. Confidence: medium (three walkthroughs). Next: ask five non-claimers and check if a simpler option exists."

*Corporate academy context.* An HR team issues a credential to employees who finish a data protection refresher. Claim is high because the credential links to the learning record, but nobody shares. The HR analyst, Tomas, runs a five-question survey. Many answers say "I did not know where I would use it" and "my manager never mentioned it". He writes: "Weak point C and D. Data: high claim, low share. Research: no clear use. Confidence: low (survey only). Next: interview three line managers."

*All figures and findings are illustrative.*

**Practice activity: Build a drop-off table**

Use your journey map from module 1 and either your own programme data or the illustrative funnel above.

1. Write down the definitions of claim rate and share rate you will use, with the time window and the group.
2. Calculate the step-to-step rates for at least four stages. If you have no data, use the illustrative funnel.
3. Identify the two weakest points.
4. For each, design one research method from the list above. Write three questions you would ask, or the task you would set in a walkthrough.
5. Fill in a drop-off table with four columns: what the data shows, likely cause, confidence, and what you need to learn next.
6. Add one ethical safeguard (for example, how you ask for consent or protect names).

## Interventions

### Matching an intervention to a drop-off

An **intervention** is a change you make to the journey to improve a result. Choose interventions that fit the cause you found. A weak message needs better writing. A hard claim step needs a simpler path. A missing use case needs a conversation with employers or managers.

Group interventions into four kinds:

- **Information.** Tell earners what the credential is, what it can be used for and what happens next. Do this early, not only at issue.
- **Timing.** Send messages when earners can act. Avoid times when they are busy or away.
- **Ease.** Remove or simplify steps. Offer clear instructions. Provide help in the place where earners get stuck.
- **Value.** Show where the credential is used and recognised. Give earners concrete options for sharing, such as a profile section, a CV line or a link for an application.

Pick one main change per weak point. Several changes at once make it hard to learn which one worked. You will see why in the next module.

### Communication that earners act on

A good message is short, clear and honest. Use this structure:

1. **Why you are writing.** One sentence that names the credential and the achievement.
2. **What to do.** One main action, with a clear button or link.
3. **What it takes.** Say how long it takes and whether an account is needed.
4. **What it is for.** One or two concrete uses.
5. **Help.** A named way to get support.

Write in plain language. Avoid jargon such as "interoperable" or "verifiable" unless the earner needs it. Do not use pressure or false urgency. Do not promise outcomes the credential cannot deliver, such as a job or a pay rise.

Send a short series, not one message. A simple series might include an early mention during the programme, an issue message, a reminder after a week and a "how to use it" message after the claim. Keep each message to one purpose. Respect opt-out choices and your organisation's rules on email.

### Support that removes friction

Support means the help earners get when something is unclear. Design it as part of the journey.

- **A short guide.** One page with screenshots or steps for claiming and sharing. Test it with a real earner.
- **A named contact.** A person or team and a typical response time.
- **Help at the point of need.** A link in the message, a short note on the claim page, or a tutor who knows the process.
- **Support for managers and tutors.** A short note that explains the credential, so they can ask about it and recognise it.
- **Accessibility.** Messages and pages that work with screen readers, have enough contrast and use plain language. Offer an alternative route if someone cannot use the main one.

Think about where earners will store the credential. Today many earners use a credential platform account. In the European Union, the EUDI Wallet is an emerging option under eIDAS 2.0. Member States must offer at least one wallet by 24 December 2026. Germany plans its state wallet ("d-you") for 2 January 2027, with basic functions first. Regulated private sectors must accept the wallet about one year after issuance starts. Readiness varies widely between Member States. Do not promise earners that a wallet will hold their credential unless your tools and the wallet support it. Status as of November 2026; check before publishing.

### Checking AI-written messages

Many teams use AI tools to draft earner messages. This can save time. It also brings risks. An AI tool can produce fluent text that is wrong, too pushy or unsuited to the audience. You remain responsible for every message you send.

Check each AI-written message on two points:

**Tone.**
- Does it sound like your organisation?
- Is it respectful and free of pressure and false urgency?
- Is the language plain and suitable for the audience?
- Does it avoid hype and promises about jobs or pay?

**Accuracy.**
- Is the credential name, date and programme correct?
- Are the steps to claim correct for your current tools?
- Are claims about recognition, standards or laws true and checkable?
- Are links, contact details and deadlines correct?
- Does it avoid inventing statistics, quotes or endorsements?

Do not paste personal data about earners into an AI tool unless your organisation has approved the tool and the use. Keep a person in the loop: a named reviewer checks and approves before sending. Keep a record of the prompt, the draft and the approved version for later review. Test the final message with at least one real reader.

## Testing and measuring

### The test-and-learn loop

You now have interventions. Before you roll them out to everyone, test them. A test shows whether a change helps, harms or does nothing, and it protects earners from poorly tested changes.

The **test-and-learn loop** has five steps:

1. **Observe.** Find a weak point with data and research.
2. **Hypothesise.** Write what you expect and why. Use the form: "If we change X for group G, then measure M will improve, because of cause C."
3. **Test.** Run the change on a limited group for a set time.
4. **Measure.** Compare the result with a baseline or a comparison group.
5. **Decide and learn.** Adopt, adjust or drop the change. Record what you learned.

Then return to step 1. Each loop gives you evidence for the next one.

### Designing a fair test

A test is only useful if you can trust what it shows. Follow these principles.

- **Compare like with like.** Use a **comparison group** that receives the old version. Split earners by a simple rule, such as alternating names in a list or by cohort, so that the groups are similar. If you cannot split a cohort, compare with the previous cohort and note the limits of that comparison.
- **Change one thing.** If the new message and the new claim page start together, you cannot tell which helped.
- **Set the measure first.** Choose the **primary measure** before the test starts, for example claim rate within 14 days. Add one or two **guardrail measures**, such as support requests or opt-outs, to check that the change does not cause harm.
- **Set the window.** Decide how long the test runs and when you will read the result.
- **Consider size.** With small groups, differences can be due to chance. If your cohort has 40 earners, a rise of three claims may mean little. Treat small tests as signals that need repeating, and say so in your report. If you need a formal decision, ask a colleague with statistical skills to advise.
- **Respect earners.** Do not give one group a clearly worse experience on purpose. Do not withhold essential information. Make sure all earners can still claim. Follow your organisation's rules on testing with people.

Write a one-page **test plan** before you start. It should state the hypothesis, the groups, the change, the primary and guardrail measures, the window and the decision rule, for example "adopt if claim rate within 14 days is at least 10 percentage points higher and support requests do not rise".

### Reading and reporting results

When the window closes, compute the primary measure for each group. Check the guardrail measures. Then write down three things: what happened, what you think it means and how sure you are.

Be careful with common errors:

- **Reading too much into small numbers.** Report counts as well as percentages.
- **Stopping early** because the early result looks good.
- **Ignoring outside events.** A deadline, a holiday or a manager's reminder may have changed behaviour.
- **Measuring only the easy thing.** A high share rate means little if no one views the shared credential. Where your tools allow it, look at downstream signals such as verified views, and at what earners say about use.
- **Treating a rise in a rate as proof of value.** The measures show behaviour with the credential. They do not show competence or career outcomes by themselves.

Report results in a short, plain format: the question, the test, the result with counts, the decision and the next step. Share what did not work as well as what did. Record decisions so that new colleagues can see the history.

Finally, set up a light **monitoring routine**: review the key rates each cohort, keep the journey map up to date and record changes. Metrics belong to a plan. The plan states who looks at them, how often and what triggers action.

**Worked example: A test of a reminder message**

*University context.* Amara has 160 earners in the next cohort. She plans to test a new reminder message with a short guide. She splits the list alternately by surname order into two groups of 80. Group A gets the old reminder. Group B gets the new reminder and guide. Her hypothesis: "If we send a plain-language reminder with a guide to group B, then claim rate within 14 days will rise, because earners stopped at unclear steps." Primary measure: claim rate within 14 days. Guardrails: support emails and opt-outs. Decision rule: adopt if group B is at least 10 percentage points higher and guardrails do not worsen. *Illustrative result:* group A 28 of 80 claimed (35 per cent), group B 41 of 80 claimed (51 per cent). Support emails fall slightly. Amara adopts the change, notes that the groups are small and plans to repeat the test with the next cohort.

*Corporate academy context.* Jonas cannot split a single cohort because all employees on a site attend together. He compares two sites with similar profiles: site 1 keeps the old Friday afternoon email, site 2 receives the message on a Tuesday morning with a note to managers. He states the limit: sites may differ in other ways. Primary measure: claim rate within 14 days. Guardrail: complaints to HR. *Illustrative result:* site 1: 30 of 75 (40 per cent). Site 2: 45 of 80 (56 per cent). Jonas records that the result is a signal, since the sites differ, and plans a second comparison on a different pair of sites before a company-wide change.

*All numbers are illustrative.*

The assessor reviews the evidence against the rubric. When you pass, ICoBC issues the badge. The badge shows that you completed these tasks in a controlled setting at the Ability stage. It does not prove competence in any job.

**Assessment practice.** Assessors should use the rubric as written and give written feedback on each criterion. Use at least one other assessor to check a sample of submissions for consistency. Remind participants not to submit personal data about real earners.

**Adjustments.** If a participant has no access to real programme data, allow the use of the illustrative funnel or a case from this course, provided that all invented figures are labelled.

**Time.** Plan about 90 minutes for participants to draft the task during the workshop and allow time after the session to finish it.
